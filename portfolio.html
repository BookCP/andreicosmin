Case Study 1 — The Help Center Redesign

Replacing IT's mental model with the user's intent

The legacy portal forced employees to navigate IT categories before they could describe their problem. A user with a broken Google login had to decide: "Is this a Google issue or a Fix an Issue issue?" This created decision fatigue before the employee could even type.

Problem: 45% of tickets landed in catch-all categories. ~35 tickets per quarter were misrouted, adding 0.5–1 business day to resolution. Agents spent significant time in follow-ups asking for context the form should have captured.

What I analysed

I reviewed 283 customer-facing tickets from January–March 2026. The data showed that users do not think in IT categories. They think in symptoms and outcomes: "I cannot open my file" or "I need to get into this tool."

What I designed

I replaced 15+ legacy request types with six intent-based forms. Each form is organised by what the user is trying to do, not by which IT system is involved. The design was grounded in behavioural research:

Principle Research Basis How I Applied It
Recognition over Recall Nielsen's Heuristic #6 Dropdowns and radio buttons for all finite answer sets; free text only for 200+ item lists
Hick's Law Decision time increases with choices; threshold ~10–15 items Maximum 6–7 options per question
Progressive Disclosure Multi-step forms outperform single long forms by 10–20% Maximum 3–4 visible fields at a time; forms unfold based on answers
Decision Fatigue Quality degrades after 8–12 questions for frustrated users No path exceeds 7 questions; break-fix form capped at 5

The six forms

Form User's Mental State Why Separate
🔧 Something isn't working "It WAS working, now it is NOT" Frustrated user. Shortest path. Incident workflow.
🔑 I need access "I need to get into something" Highest volume (34.6%). Unique approval workflows.
💿 I need software "I need a tool I don't have" Security assessment gate. Procurement workflow.
⚙️ Set something up "I need IT to configure something" Configuration requests. Different from access or software.
💻 I need hardware "I need a physical thing" Physical fulfilment. Full device lifecycle.
❓ I have a question "I'm not sure what I need" Catch-all. Feeds learning loop. Reduces "Other" elsewhere.
Outcome: The design eliminated ITIL jargon from the user interface. "SSO" became "connect a tool with DeepL login." "P1/P2/P3" became "completely blocked / slowed down / minor annoyance." ITIL categories are still applied — but automatically, post-submission, invisible to the user.

Case Study 2 — The Escalation & Collaboration Model

Designing the internal operating model, not just the customer-facing portal

The new Help Center redesigned what the user sees. This proposal redesigned what happens after submission — how tickets flow between teams, how ownership transfers, and how we measure performance fairly.

Problem: 94.4% of tickets had no level label. There was no formal escalation path between L1, L2, and L3. When we escalated, it happened in Slack DMs and nothing was recorded. Performance was measured by ticket count, which penalised anyone who took on complex work.

Core principle

Escalation is a routing decision, not an admission of failure. It means: "this ticket needs expertise or access that lives in another team." It does not mean "I couldn't handle it."

The workflow I designed

┌─────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ ┌──────────────────┐ ┌──────────┐ │ Triage │────▶│ In Progress │────▶│ Pending Specialist│────▶│ Resolved │ │ (L1) │ │ (L1 owns) │ │ (L2 or L3 owns) │ │ │ └─────────┘ └──────────────┘ └──────────────────┘ └──────────┘ │ ▲ │ │ │ │ (de-escalation) │ └──────────────────────┘ │ └────▶ Waiting for Customer / Approval ─────▶ (back to In Progress)

SLA clock management

The most contentious part of any escalation model is who gets blamed for delays. I designed the SLA clocks to pause and resume based on team ownership, not ticket creation:

SLA Starts when Pauses when
L1 Resolution Status = In Progress, Responsible Team = IT Support Pending Specialist, Waiting for Customer, or Waiting for Approval
L2 Resolution Responsible Team = Platform, status = Pending Specialist Waiting for Customer, Waiting for Approval, or de-escalated back to L1
L3 Resolution Responsible Team = Automation, status = Pending Specialist Same as L2

Fair performance tracking

I proposed replacing the single "tickets closed" metric with a balanced scorecard. If you only count tickets, you reward picking easy work and penalising anyone who takes on complex problems or writes documentation.

Metric What it captures
Tickets resolved Productivity — still important, just not the only thing
Complexity distribution What types of work someone handles (Standard / Extended / Advanced)
Escalation rate % escalated — low rate means resolving more independently
Contributions to L2/L3 tickets Stretch work tracked via a Contributing Analysts field
Knowledge articles created Prevents repeat tickets, builds team capability
CSAT Quality of resolution, not just speed

Case Study 3 — The IAMJAM Engine

The cross-tool workflow that governed access for 1,200+ employees

My most invisible but highest-leverage work was the Identity & Access Management pipeline. Every Phoenix, Vault, GitHub, Copilot, and ClickHouse access request at DeepL passed through a workflow I operated daily. It ran across five tools, but the same piece of work was measured in each system separately.

The flow

Tool / Step What happens
Slack User pings #help-it: "I need access to Phoenix" or "GitHub Copilot license please"
Jira (ISE) OASyS service desk creates a ticket. SLA clock starts.
GitLab jira-webhook automation generates an access-request MR modifying the YAML config file
GitLab review I review the YAML diff, approve as teamlead/resource owner, and merge
AE-DIR / LDAP CI pipeline propagates the change. The user gets the access.
Jira (auto-close) Ticket auto-closes on MR merge. Resolution: Done.
Why this matters: The 681 Jira tickets and 142 GitLab commits are not separate work — they are the same work measured in two systems. My contribution was the human approval decision in step 4: judging whether the requested access was organisationally appropriate. 137 access changes over 24 months, every one checked by hand.

Case Study 4 — The Workshop & Data Analysis

Using data to drive operational decisions

In March 2026 I facilitated a strategic workshop with my manager and team to align on how we worked, what we owned, and where we were going. I did not run it on opinions. I ran it on one year of ticket data.

Key findings I presented

Finding Implication
94.4% of tickets had no level label We could not measure how much work was L1 vs L2 vs L3
10.6% of tickets sat unassigned No auto-assignment or round-robin existed; agents self-assigned
Priority changed on only 5.5% of tickets, mostly downgraded Users overstated urgency less than we assumed
Volume grew +24% YoY but team size did not We needed process improvement, not just more people
No shared dashboards existed The team had no visibility into ticket health

The action points I proposed

  1. Define team scope & responsibility matrix
  2. Standardise triage & distribution with assignment rules
  3. Build the escalation workflow (see Case Study 2)
  4. Review and redefine SLAs against actual performance
  5. Build ITSM dashboards embedded in Confluence
  6. Implement Jira Asset Management with JAMF/Intune sync
  7. Automate offboarding (device lock, backup, collection)
  8. Fix onboarding data flow with the People Team
  9. Create shared playbooks for recurring tasks
  10. Align access levels and map skill gaps for cross-coverage
