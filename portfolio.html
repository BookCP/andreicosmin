Case Study 1 — The Help Center Redesign

Replacing IT's mental model with the user's intent

The legacy portal forced employees to navigate IT categories before they could describe their problem. A user with a broken Google login had to decide: "Is this a Google issue or a Fix an Issue issue?" This created decision fatigue before the employee could even type.

Problem: 45% of tickets landed in catch-all categories. ~35 tickets per quarter were misrouted, adding 0.5–1 business day to resolution. Agents spent significant time in follow-ups asking for context the form should have captured.

The analysis

The analysis covered 283 customer-facing tickets from January–March 2026. The data showed that users do not think in IT categories. They think in symptoms and outcomes: "I cannot open my file" or "I need to get into this tool."

The design

The framework replaced 15+ legacy request types with six intent-based forms. Each form is organised by what the user is trying to do, not by which IT system is involved. The design was grounded in behavioural research:

PrincipleResearch BasisApplication
Recognition over RecallNielsen's Heuristic #6Dropdowns and radio buttons for all finite answer sets; free text only for 200+ item lists
Hick's LawDecision time increases logarithmically with choices; threshold ~10–15 itemsMaximum 6–7 options per question
Progressive DisclosureMulti-step forms outperform single long forms by 10–20%Maximum 3–4 visible fields at a time; forms unfold based on answers
Decision FatigueQuality degrades after 8–12 questions for frustrated usersNo path exceeds 7 questions; break-fix form capped at 5

Status: the framework was fully designed, but ultimately not implemented.

The six forms

FormUser's Mental StateWhy Separate
🔧 Something isn't working"It WAS working, now it is NOT"Frustrated user. Shortest path. Incident workflow.
🔑 I need access"I need to get into something"Highest volume (34.6%). Unique approval workflows.
💿 I need software"I need a tool I don't have"Security assessment gate. Procurement workflow.
⚙️ Set something up"I need IT to configure something"Configuration requests. Different from access or software.
💻 I need hardware"I need a physical thing"Physical fulfilment. Full device lifecycle.
❓ I have a question"I'm not sure what I need"Catch-all. Feeds learning loop. Reduces "Other" elsewhere.
The design: ITIL jargon disappears from the user interface. "SSO" becomes "connect a tool with DeepL login." "P1/P2/P3" becomes "completely blocked / slowed down / minor annoyance." ITIL categories are still applied — automatically, post-submission, invisible to the user.

Case Study 2 — The Escalation & Collaboration Model

Designing the internal operating model, not just the customer-facing portal

The new Help Center redesigned what the user sees. This proposal redesigned what happens after submission — how tickets flow between teams, how ownership transfers, and how performance is measured fairly.

Status: draft proposal, open for team feedback (April 2026).

Problem: There was no formal escalation path between L1, L2, and L3 — escalation happened informally, over Slack or verbally, and nothing was recorded. Level labels had been introduced about two months earlier as a quick, lightweight categorisation method, and the data showed the gap: 94.4% of tickets from the previous 12 months had no level label, largely because they pre-dated the labels. Labels also required manual input, and the team was not aligned on what counted as L1 versus L2. Performance was measured by ticket count, which penalised anyone who took on complex work.

Core principle

Escalation is a routing decision, not an admission of failure. It means: "this ticket needs expertise or access that lives in another team." It does not mean "I couldn't handle it."

The workflow

Triage L1 reviews & assigns
→
In Progress Assigned agent works
→
Pending Specialist Ticket is with L2 or L3
→
Resolved By whichever team resolves it
↕
Waiting for Approval SLA clocks pause · back to In Progress once decided
↕
Waiting for Customer SLA clocks pause · blocked on requester input

De-escalation: Pending Specialist → In Progress, with a mandatory feedback comment.

SLA clock management

The most sensitive part of any escalation model is how SLA time is attributed when tickets move between teams. The proposal pauses and resumes SLA clocks based on team ownership, not ticket creation:

SLAStarts whenPauses when
L1 ResolutionStatus = In Progress, Responsible Team = IT SupportPending Specialist, Waiting for Customer, or Waiting for Approval
L2 ResolutionResponsible Team = Platform, status = Pending SpecialistWaiting for Customer, Waiting for Approval, or de-escalated back to L1
L3 ResolutionResponsible Team = Automation, status = Pending SpecialistSame as L2

Fair performance tracking

The proposal was to replace the single "tickets closed" metric with a balanced scorecard. Counting only tickets rewards picking easy work and penalises anyone who takes on complex problems or writes documentation.

Case Study 3 — The IAMJAM Engine

The cross-tool workflow behind access management

Alongside day-to-day support, a recurring part of the work was the Identity & Access Management pipeline. Every Phoenix, Vault, GitHub, Copilot, and ClickHouse access request at DeepL passed through it. It ran across five tools, but the same piece of work was measured in two systems: Jira and GitLab.

The flow

Tool / StepWhat happens
SlackUser asks in #help-it: "I need access to Phoenix" or "GitHub Copilot license please"
Jira (ISE)A ticket is created via the OASyS service desk. SLA clock starts.
GitLabAn access-request MR modifying the YAML config file is prepared and pushed
GitLab approvalApproval is requested from the responsible teamlead / resource owner; once approved, the MR is merged
AE-DIR / LDAPThe CI pipeline propagates the change. The user gets the access.
Jira (auto-close)Ticket auto-closes on MR merge. Resolution: Done.
Why this matters: The 681 Jira tickets and 142 GitLab commits are not separate work — they are the same work measured in two systems. 137 access changes over 24 months, each one checked by hand and routed for approval.
